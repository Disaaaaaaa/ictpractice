# Plagiarism
@lo 12.1.3.5

When students write documentation for a project — a report, a user guide, a technical description — they use many sources: web pages, books, other people's code. Using sources is normal and good; **hiding** that you used them is **plagiarism**.

In grade 12 this topic belongs to **creating documentation**. A system that is developed for a client is delivered with documents: a **technical specification**, a **user guide**, a **test report**, a **project report** for the school. These documents are judged as **your** work — by a teacher, an examiner or a client who pays for them. If parts of them were written by someone else and this is not shown, the reader is misled about what you did and what you know. That is why checking documents for plagiarism is part of producing professional documentation, not only a school rule.

## Using sources honestly

There is nothing wrong with **using** sources. Every good report builds on other people's work: a definition from a textbook, a statistic from a government website, a code example from the documentation of a library. The difference between research and plagiarism is **acknowledgement**. When the reader can see **which** words or ideas came from **which** source, the work is honest; when the borrowed material is mixed in without a sign, it is plagiarism — even if the writer did not mean to cheat.

Plagiarism can be **deliberate** (copying a whole essay to save time) or **accidental** (forgetting where a note came from, or not knowing how to paraphrase). Schools and universities usually treat both seriously, because the result for the reader is the same: the work is not what it claims to be. The best protection against accidental plagiarism is **good habits while researching** — recording every source and marking copied text in your notes — and **checking the final document** before handing it in.

Plagiarism is not only about words. A diagram redrawn from a website, a table of data, a photograph, a piece of music in a video, a program, even the **structure** of someone else's argument can all be plagiarised. Translating a text from Russian or English into Kazakh does not make it yours either: the ideas and the order of the content still belong to the original author.

:::definition Plagiarism
**Presenting another person's work, words, ideas, images or code as your own**, without clearly **acknowledging the source**. It is a form of **academic dishonesty** and can also break **copyright** law.
:::

:::definition Source and citation
A **source** is any work you take information from (book, article, web page, video, program). A **citation (reference)** tells the reader **who** created the source, **when**, and **where** to find it.
:::

:::definition Academic integrity
Doing your work **honestly**: your own words and ideas, with **every source acknowledged**, and following the rules of the task (e.g. whether AI tools or help from others are allowed).
:::

:::compare Forms of plagiarism
| Form | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct (copy-paste)** | Text is copied **word for word** without quotation marks or a reference | A paragraph from Wikipedia pasted into an essay |
| **Mosaic / patchwriting** | Phrases from several sources are **mixed** together with a few words changed | Sentences from three websites stitched together |
| **Paraphrasing without citation** | Someone else's **idea** is rewritten in new words, but the source is **not given** | An article's argument retold with no reference |
| **Translation plagiarism** | A text in another language is **translated** and presented as original | An English article translated into Kazakh |
| **Self-plagiarism** | Re-using **your own earlier work** without permission or acknowledgement | Last year's project submitted for a new task |
| **Code plagiarism** | Copying program code and only changing **variable names / comments** | A classmate's Python game with renamed variables |
| **Ghost-writing / AI text** | Work written by **another person** or an **AI tool** submitted as your own, when this is not allowed | An essay generated by a chatbot |
| **Incorrect citation** | A **wrong or incomplete** source, so the reader cannot find the original | "Source: Internet" |
:::

The forms differ in **how much** is taken and **how well it is hidden**, but they all share one feature: the reader is not told the truth about the source. Direct copying is the easiest to detect; mosaic plagiarism and translation are harder, because the exact words have changed. Self-plagiarism surprises many students: you are allowed to build on your own earlier work, but you must say that you are doing so, and you must have the teacher's agreement if the task asks for **new** work.

:::cards
### Why plagiarism is a problem
- It is **dishonest** — the work does not show the student's own knowledge.
- It is **unfair** to the original author and to other students.
- The student **does not learn** the skills the task was designed to develop.
- It can **break copyright** — the author's rights are ignored.
### Consequences
- At school: **zero marks**, the work must be redone, a note to parents.
- In exams and competitions: **disqualification**.
- At university: a failed course or **expulsion**; degrees can be withdrawn.
- At work: **loss of reputation**, dismissal, **legal action** for copyright infringement.
### Why people plagiarise
- Lack of time / poor planning.
- Not knowing how to **cite** or **paraphrase** correctly.
- Thinking that information on the Internet is **free to use**.
- Pressure to get high marks.
:::

:::warning Common mistakes
- "I changed some words, so it is mine" — it is still **patchwriting / plagiarism** without a citation.
- "It was on the Internet, so anyone can use it" — web content is **protected by copyright** too.
- Forgetting that **ideas, images, tables and code** can be plagiarised, not only text.
- Thinking that re-using your **own** old work is always fine — without permission it is **self-plagiarism**.
:::

# How anti-plagiarism resources work
@lo 12.1.3.5

:::definition Anti-plagiarism resource
A program or online service that **compares a document with a very large database of texts** — web pages, books, journals and previously submitted student work — and produces a **similarity report** showing **which parts match other sources** and **how much** of the document matches.
:::

:::definition Similarity score (similarity index)
The **percentage of the document's text that matches** text in the checker's databases. **Originality** = 100 % − similarity. It measures **matching text**, not plagiarism.
:::

## Why a checker is needed

A teacher who marks 60 reports cannot remember every web page, book and earlier student paper on the topic. A computer can store **billions** of documents and compare a new text with all of them in seconds. Anti-plagiarism resources therefore do the part of the job that computers are good at — **finding matching text quickly in a huge collection** — and leave the part that needs judgement — **deciding whether the matching is acceptable** — to a person.

## Fragments and fingerprints

Comparing a whole document with millions of others word by word would be far too slow. Checkers therefore **normalise** the text first: they convert it to lower case, remove punctuation and often remove very common words ("the", "and", "of"). Then they cut it into **overlapping fragments** of a fixed number of words, called **shingles** (n-grams). Each fragment is turned by a **hash function** into a short number, its **fingerprint**. Two identical fragments always give the same fingerprint, so finding matching text becomes the fast job of finding **equal numbers** in an index — just as a library catalogue finds a book by its code without reading every shelf.

Because the fragments **overlap**, copying even one sentence produces several matching fingerprints in a row, and the checker can mark the whole copied passage. Changing a single word breaks only the fragments that contain that word; the others still match. That is why "changing a few words" rarely hides copying. Some modern checkers also compare **meaning**, not just exact words, so that synonyms and reordered sentences can be detected, and some can compare a text with sources in **other languages**.

## The databases

A checker can only find a match with a text that is in its **databases**. These are usually of three kinds: **the public Internet** (crawled web pages), **published material** (books, journals, conference papers, often through agreements with publishers) and an **archive of documents submitted before** to that service. The archive is the reason institutional services can find copying between students: when a student submits a report, it is stored, and next year's copy of it will match. Free online checkers usually search only the web, so they cannot see a classmate's work or a printed book.

## Checking program code

Program code is checked differently. Renaming variables, changing comments or reordering functions does not change what a program **does**, so code checkers such as **MOSS** and **JPlag** first convert the program into a sequence of **tokens** (keyword, identifier, operator, number…) and compare the **structure**, not the names. Two programs that differ only in variable names give the same token sequence and are reported as similar.

:::steps How a document is checked
- The user (or teacher) **uploads** the document (DOCX, PDF, TXT) or pastes the text.
- The system breaks the text into **fragments** (overlapping sequences of words, "shingles") and creates a **digital fingerprint (hash)** of each.
- The fingerprints are **compared** with its databases: the Internet, academic publications, its **archive of submitted papers**.
- **Matching fragments are highlighted**, and each match is linked to its **source**.
- A **similarity (originality) score** in % is calculated, and a **report** is produced for the user to **interpret**.
:::

:::example Worked example — how fingerprints find a copied sentence
*A source on the web contains the sentence* "A primary key uniquely identifies each record in a table". *A student's report contains* "In our database a primary key uniquely identifies every record in a table".

1. **Normalise** both: lower case, no punctuation → *a primary key uniquely identifies each record in a table* and *in our database a primary key uniquely identifies every record in a table*.
2. **Shingles of 3 words** from the source: *a primary key*, *primary key uniquely*, *key uniquely identifies*, *uniquely identifies each*, *identifies each record*, *each record in*, *record in a*, *in a table*.
3. **Shingles of the report** include *a primary key*, *primary key uniquely*, *key uniquely identifies*, *record in a*, *in a table* — **5 of the 8** source fingerprints match. The shingles containing *every* (instead of *each*) do not match.
4. The checker **highlights** the passage and links it to the web page. Changing one word did **not** hide the copying.
5. The teacher looks at the passage: there are no quotation marks and no reference, so it must be **quoted with a citation or rewritten and cited**.
:::

## What the checker can and cannot do

An anti-plagiarism resource is a **tool for finding evidence**, not a judge. Its strengths are speed, the size of its databases and the fact that it treats every document in the same way. Its weaknesses come from the way it works: it can only match text that is **already in its databases**, it compares **words and fragments** rather than understanding ideas, and it cannot know the **rules of the task** — whether a quotation was allowed, whether the student worked in a permitted group, whether a passage is the student's own earlier work used with permission. A report must therefore always be **interpreted by a person** who knows the task and the student. Teachers use the report as a starting point for a conversation, for example by asking the student to explain a highlighted passage in their own words.

:::cards
### Limitations of anti-plagiarism software
- Cannot detect copying from **sources not in its database** (printed books, private documents).
- Can be fooled by heavy **paraphrasing**, synonym-replacing tools or **translation**.
- Does not check **images, diagrams** or **ideas** well.
- **False positives** — common phrases, names, standard definitions, the reference list.
- Uploaded work may be **stored** by the service — privacy of the student's own work.
- A human must always **interpret** the report.
### What the checker cannot decide
- Whether a match is **properly quoted and cited**.
- Whether the use is **allowed** by the task rules.
- Whether the student **understood** the work.
:::

# Checking a document and reading the report
@lo 12.1.3.5

The main purpose of checking your **own** document is to catch problems **before** someone else does. Students often discover that a sentence they thought was their own is almost identical to a website they read weeks ago, or that a quotation lost its quotation marks when it was pasted. A check gives time to correct these honestly.

## The parts of a similarity report

Most reports have the same parts. The **overall similarity score** in per cent is shown at the top. Below it is a **list of sources**, each with its own percentage — the share of the document that matches that one source — usually in order from the largest. The **document itself** is shown with matching passages **highlighted**, often in a different colour for each source, so you can click a passage and see the original next to it. **Filters** let you exclude quoted text, the bibliography and very short matches (for example, fewer than 8 words), and the score is recalculated. Some services also show an **AI-writing indicator**; like the similarity score, it is a signal to investigate, not proof.

## Judging each match

Reading a report means asking the same three questions about every highlighted passage. **Is it acknowledged?** A quotation in quotation marks with a reference is honest, even if it matches 100 %. **Is it necessary?** A report that is 40 % correct quotations is honest but shows little of the student's own work, and may get a low mark for that reason. **Is it common language?** Phrases such as "the aim of this project is" or standard technical terms match many sources and are not plagiarism. Only matches that are **not acknowledged** and **not common language** are real problems.

:::steps Checking your own document before submitting
- Finish the **final draft**, with all quotations in **quotation marks** and a full **reference list**.
- Upload it to the school's checker (or a free one) and wait for the **report**.
- Look at the **overall score**, then at **every highlighted passage** and its source.
- Use the filters to **exclude quotations and the bibliography** — see what is left.
- Fix every problem: add quotation marks and a reference to a quotation; **cite** a paraphrase; **rewrite** text that is too close to the source.
- **Check again** and keep the final report.
:::

:::example Worked example — Aigerim's project report
*Aigerim checks her 12-page project report with the school's checker. The report shows **25 %** similarity: 12 % from a Wikipedia article, 9 % from a student paper submitted last year, 4 % from her textbook.*

1. **Wikipedia, 12 %** — the passage is a definition she copied **without quotation marks**. She either puts it in **quotation marks with a reference** or **paraphrases** it in her own words **and cites** the article.
2. **Student paper, 9 %** — she remembers using her older brother's report. This is **plagiarism of another student's work**: she must **rewrite** the section herself.
3. **Textbook, 4 %** — a short quotation **already in quotation marks** and in the reference list. This is **acceptable**; the filter "exclude quotes" removes it from the score.
4. After the changes and a **second check** the similarity is **6 %**, made of common technical phrases and the reference list.

:::cards
### Interpreting the score correctly
- A similarity score is **not** a plagiarism score — it only shows **matching text**.
- Correctly **quoted and referenced** passages, the **bibliography**, common phrases and technical terms also produce matches.
- Most checkers can **exclude quotes and the reference list** from the score.
- A **high score with no citations** needs investigation; a **low score** can still hide paraphrased or translated plagiarism.
- Many schools set a **threshold** (e.g. above 20–30 % is checked in detail), but every match is judged by a person.
### Ways of checking without a dedicated service
- Search a suspicious sentence **in quotation marks** in a search engine.
- Use **reverse image search** to find the source of a picture.
- Compare two programs with a **code-similarity tool** (MOSS, JPlag).
- Compare the **style** of a passage with the student's other work.
:::

:::warning Common mistakes
- Treating the similarity % as **proof** of plagiarism, or 0 % as proof of originality.
- Using a synonym-replacer to "beat" the checker — the work is still plagiarised and often becomes unreadable.
- Checking only the **number** and never opening the highlighted passages.
- Uploading a confidential document to a free checker without reading its **privacy terms**.
:::

# Avoiding plagiarism
@lo 12.1.3.5 12.1.3.6

Avoiding plagiarism is a **skill**, not just a promise. It has three parts: deciding **how** to use each source (quote, paraphrase or summarise), **acknowledging** it in the text, and listing it in the **reference list** at the end. When all three are done, a reader — or an anti-plagiarism report — can separate your work from the work of others.

## When to quote, paraphrase or summarise

**Quote** when the exact words matter: a legal definition, a striking statement, the words of an interview, a line of a standard. Quotations should be short and few; a report built from quotations shows that the writer collected material but did not process it. **Paraphrase** when you need one specific idea in detail but the original wording is not special — this is the most common choice in technical documentation, and it shows that you understood the source. **Summarise** when you need only the main points of a long text, such as the conclusion of a 20-page report in two sentences.

In every case the **citation** stays. Students often believe that once the words are their own, the source can be dropped. It cannot: the **idea, the data or the structure** still came from someone else. The only exception is **common knowledge** — facts that a typical reader knows or can find in many general sources without dispute. If you are unsure whether something is common knowledge, cite it; an extra citation is never plagiarism.

## Referencing systems

A **referencing style** is an agreed format for citations. In the **Harvard (author–date)** style the text contains the author's surname and the year in brackets, and the full details are in an alphabetical reference list. Other styles use **numbers** in square brackets [1] (IEEE, common in computing) or **footnotes**. The style matters less than **consistency** and **completeness**: the reader must be able to find the exact source. A web reference needs the author or organisation, year, title, URL and the **date you accessed it**, because web pages change. Word processors can build citations and the bibliography automatically from a list of sources, which keeps the two always matching.

:::definition Quotation
The **exact words** of a source, placed in **quotation marks** (or indented for a long quotation), followed by a **citation**.
:::

:::definition Paraphrase and summary
A **paraphrase** restates one idea **completely in your own words and sentence structure**; a **summary** gives only the **main points** of a longer text, much shorter. **Both still need a citation**, because the idea is not yours.
:::

:::definition Common knowledge
Facts that are **widely known** and found in many sources (e.g. "Astana is the capital of Kazakhstan"). They **do not need** a citation; statistics, opinions and specific findings do.
:::

:::compare Quoting, paraphrasing and summarising
| Method | How | Length compared with the original | Citation needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quotation** | The **exact words**, in **quotation marks** | The same | **Yes** — with the page if possible |
| **Paraphrase** | The idea rewritten **completely in your own words and structure** | About the same | **Yes** |
| **Summary** | The **main points**, in your own words | Much **shorter** | **Yes** |
| Common knowledge | Widely known facts | — | No |
:::

Every citation in the text has a matching entry in the **reference list**, which is in **alphabetical order** of authors.
:::

:::example Worked example — paraphrasing a passage step by step
*Original (from a web page by the Ministry of Digital Development, 2025):* "Users should create passwords of at least twelve characters that combine upper- and lower-case letters, digits and symbols, and should never reuse the same password on different services."

1. **Bad (patchwriting):** "Users ought to make passwords of at least twelve characters that mix upper- and lower-case letters, numbers and symbols, and must not reuse a password on several services." — the structure and most words are copied; a checker would highlight it.
2. **Understand and close the source.** Main ideas: long password (12+), four kinds of character, a different password for each service.
3. **Write from memory:** "A strong password is long — twelve characters or more — and mixes the four kinds of character. Using a separate password for each account means that one leaked password does not open the others (Ministry of Digital Development, 2025)."
4. **Check:** the meaning is the same, the sentences are new (the student even added the reason), and the **citation** is present.
5. Add the full entry to the reference list.
:::

:::steps How to paraphrase correctly
- Read the passage until you **understand** it; then **close** the source.
- Write the idea **from memory**, in your own words and your own sentence structure.
- Compare with the original: no long phrases copied; the **meaning** is unchanged.
- Put any unique phrase you kept in **quotation marks**.
- Add the **citation** (author, year).
:::

:::tip Good habits
Keep a list of every source **while you research** (author, title, date, URL, date accessed), write notes **in your own words**, mark copied text in your notes clearly, and use the word processor's **References** tools (citations, bibliography, footnotes). Check your own document with an anti-plagiarism resource **before** you submit it.
:::

:::warning Common mistakes
- Quotation marks **without** a citation, or a citation **without** quotation marks around copied words.
- Swapping a few words for synonyms and calling it a paraphrase — that is **patchwriting**.
- A citation in the text that is **missing from the reference list**, or "Source: Google".
- Citing "common knowledge" for statistics or someone's opinion.
:::

# Copyright protection rules
@lo 12.1.3.6

:::definition Copyright
The **exclusive legal right of the author** (or owner) of an original work — text, software, music, images, films, websites — to **copy, distribute, adapt, publish and perform** it, and to **allow or forbid** others to do so.
:::

:::definition Licence
**Permission from the copyright owner** to use a work under stated **conditions** (e.g. one computer, non-commercial use only, credit the author). It does **not** transfer ownership.
:::

:::definition Public domain
Works that are **no longer protected** by copyright (the term has expired) or whose owner gave up the rights. Anyone may use them freely.
:::

:::compare The main rules of copyright protection
| Rule | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Copyright is automatic** | A work is protected **from the moment it is created** in a material form — no registration is needed; the © sign is optional but useful |
| **The work must be original** | It is the author's **own creative work**, recorded in some form (written, saved, filmed) |
| **The author owns the rights** | **Economic rights** (to earn money from copies, licences) and **moral rights** (to be named as the author, to protect the work from distortion) |
| **Protection lasts for a limited time** | In Kazakhstan and many countries: the author's **life + 70 years**; afterwards the work enters the **public domain** |
| **Permission is needed** | To copy, publish, translate, adapt or sell a work you need the **owner's permission** or a **licence** |
| **Ideas and facts are not protected** | Copyright protects the **expression** (the text, code, image), not the idea itself |
| **Limited free use is allowed** | **Quotation** for study, criticism, news or teaching is allowed **with the author's name and source** and only to a justified extent |
| **Software is protected like a literary work** | Copying, distributing or installing software without a **licence** is illegal |
| **Licences set the conditions** | E.g. **Creative Commons**; open-source licences (GPL, MIT); a commercial **EULA** |
| **Breaking copyright has consequences** | Removal of content, fines, compensation to the owner, criminal liability in serious cases |
:::

## Why copyright exists

Creating a program, a book or a song takes time, skill and money. If anybody could copy and sell it the moment it appeared, authors would have no way to earn from their work and fewer people would create. Copyright solves this by giving the author a **temporary monopoly**: for a limited time only the author (or whoever the author licenses) may make and sell copies. After that time the work passes into the **public domain** and becomes free for everyone, so society benefits too.

## Economic and moral rights

Copyright gives the author two groups of rights. **Economic (property) rights** concern money and control: the right to **reproduce** the work, **distribute** copies, **publish**, **translate**, **adapt**, **perform or show it publicly** and to **license** these actions to others for payment. Economic rights can be **sold or transferred** — a programmer employed by a company usually transfers the rights to the code to the employer. **Moral (personal) rights** belong to the author **personally** and cannot be sold: the right to be **named as the author** (attribution), the right to keep the work from being **distorted** in a way that harms the author's reputation, and the right to decide whether to publish it at all. That is why a software company can own the economic rights to a program, but the novelist always remains the author of the novel.

## Free use — what is allowed without permission

The law allows some uses of protected works **without asking and without paying**, because they serve education, criticism or information. Typical examples are **quoting** a short passage for study, research, criticism or news, using works **for teaching** to an appropriate extent, making a **private copy** for personal use in some cases, and making a **back-up copy** of a legally owned program. Free use has conditions: the author's **name** and the **source** must be given, only a **justified amount** may be used, and the use must not harm the normal commercial use of the work. Copying a whole textbook "for study" is not free use.

## Plagiarism and copyright infringement

The two ideas overlap but are not the same. **Plagiarism** is an **ethical** offence: not acknowledging the source. **Copyright infringement** is a **legal** offence: using a protected work without permission. Copying a public-domain poem without naming the poet is plagiarism but not infringement (no one owns it any more). Publishing a full licensed photo **with** the photographer's name but **without** permission is infringement but not plagiarism. Copying a protected article into your report without quotation marks and selling the report is **both**.

- Thinking that everything on the Internet is in the **public domain**.
- Believing that crediting the author is enough to use **any** work — you also need **permission or a licence**.
- Saying that copyright protects **ideas** — it protects their **expression**.
:::

# Software licences and using digital content legally
@lo 12.1.3.6

Software is the most common kind of protected work that students in Computer Science use every day — operating systems, office suites, libraries, games, images and fonts on websites. When you buy or download software you almost never buy the program itself; you receive a **licence** that says what you may and may not do with it. Knowing the main kinds of licence is part of knowing the copyright rules, and it is needed whenever you choose software for a new system or build a website for a client.

:::definition EULA (End-User Licence Agreement)
The **contract between the software owner and the user** that is accepted when the software is installed. It states **how many devices** may use it, whether it may be **copied, modified or resold**, and the owner's **liability**.
:::

:::definition Open-source software
Software whose **source code is published** under a licence that allows anyone to **use, study, change and share** it, usually on condition that the licence and the authors' names are kept (e.g. **GPL**, **MIT**, **Apache**).
:::

:::definition Freeware and shareware
**Freeware** may be used **free of charge**, but the code is closed and it may not be changed or sold. **Shareware** may be **tried free** for a limited time or with limited features; after that the user must **pay** for a licence.
:::

:::definition Software piracy
**Copying, installing, distributing or selling software without a valid licence** — for example, installing one licence on 30 school computers, or downloading a "cracked" copy. It is copyright infringement.
:::

## Copyleft and permissive licences

Open-source licences come in two families. A **copyleft** licence such as the **GPL** says: you may use, change and share this code, but any program you **distribute** that includes it must also be released under the **GPL**, with its source code. The freedom is "passed on" to every later user. A **permissive** licence such as **MIT** or **Apache** asks only that you **keep the copyright notice** and the licence text; you may include the code even in a closed commercial product. When a developer builds a system for a client, the choice matters: putting GPL code into a closed product that is sold would break the licence, while MIT code can be used if the notice is kept.

## Licences in a school or company

An organisation must keep a record of the licences it owns and make sure that the number of installations does not exceed them. A **single-user licence** covers one person or device; a **multi-user (volume) licence** covers a stated number; a **site licence** covers every computer at one site, such as a whole school; a **subscription** gives the right to use the software only while payments continue. Installing more copies than the licence allows is piracy even if the school paid for some of them.

:::example Worked example — choosing software for the school robotics club
*The robotics club has 15 laptops and no budget. It needs an office suite for its reports, an image editor for its website and a programming environment. A student suggests downloading a "cracked" copy of a paid image editor.*

1. **Cracked copy** — illegal (software piracy); it may also contain **malware**. Rejected.
2. **Office suite** — **LibreOffice** (open source, free, may be installed on all 15 laptops).
3. **Image editor** — **GIMP** (open source) or a freeware editor whose licence allows **non-commercial / educational** use; read the licence for the number of devices.
4. **Programming** — **Python** and **Thonny** (open source).
5. **Club website** — images under **CC BY**: credit each author on the page; icons from a library under the **MIT** licence: keep the notice in the code.
6. Record every program and its licence in the club's **licence register**.
:::

:::warning Common mistakes
- "Free" means "no copyright" — freeware is free to **use**, but it is still protected: you may not change or sell it.
- "Open source" means "no rules" — open-source licences have **conditions** (keep the notice; GPL: share changes under the GPL).
- Buying one licence and installing it on the whole class.
- Thinking that a **trial** licence of shareware may be used for ever by reinstalling it.
:::

# Exam practice and summary
@lo 12.1.3.5 12.1.3.6

:::example Exam-style questions with model answers
**Q: Name two copyright protection rules. [2]**
Any two: copyright arises **automatically** when the work is created (1); copying / publishing needs the **owner's permission / licence** (1); quotations must name the **author and source**; protection lasts for the author's **life + 70 years**; ideas are not protected, only their expression.

**Q: Explain the difference between quoting and paraphrasing. [2]**
A quotation uses the **exact words** in quotation marks (1); a paraphrase restates the idea **in your own words**; both need a citation (1).

**Q: A student finds a photograph on a website and wants to use it in a project report. Describe what the student must do to use it legally. [3]**
Check the **licence / terms** of the photo (1); if it is not free (e.g. no CC licence), ask the **owner for permission** (1); follow the licence conditions and **credit / cite** the author and source (1).

**Q: Explain why copyright protects software. [2]**
Software is an **original creative work** of its authors (1); without protection it could be copied and sold freely, and the authors would lose income / control (1).

**Q: State what is meant by the public domain. [1]**
Works no longer protected by copyright / free for anyone to use (1).
:::

:::warning Common mistakes in answers
- Mixing up **plagiarism** (an ethical / academic problem: not acknowledging the source) and **copyright infringement** (a legal problem: using without permission). Both can happen at once.
- "Copyright lasts forever" — it lasts for a **limited time**.
- Answering "paraphrasing means changing some words" — the whole idea is rewritten **and** cited.
:::
