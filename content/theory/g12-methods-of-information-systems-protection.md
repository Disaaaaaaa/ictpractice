# Ethical problems of computer system cracking
@lo 12.1.3.1

:::definition Hacking (cracking)
Gaining **unauthorised access** to a computer system or data, usually by exploiting weaknesses or stolen passwords.
:::

:::cards
### Black-hat hacker
Breaks in illegally for profit, damage or spying.
### White-hat (ethical) hacker
Tests systems **with permission** (penetration testing) to find and fix weaknesses.
### Grey-hat hacker
Breaks in without permission but without malicious intent — still illegal.
:::

:::compare Ethical and social problems
| Problem | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Invasion of privacy | Personal messages, photos and medical data are exposed |
| Theft | Money, identities, intellectual property and trade secrets are stolen |
| Damage and disruption | Data deleted or altered; hospitals, banks or schools cannot work |
| Loss of trust | Customers stop using an organisation that was hacked |
| Responsibility | Who pays for the damage? Was the organisation negligent in protecting data? |
| "Hacking for good" | Is exposing a vulnerability without permission ethical if it helps fix it? |
| Legal consequences | Unauthorised access is a **criminal offence** (Criminal Code of the Republic of Kazakhstan; similar laws worldwide) |
:::

# Protecting data from unauthorised access
@lo 12.1.2.3

:::cards
### Strong passwords and policies
At least 12 characters, a mix of letters, digits and symbols; not reused; changed after a breach; account lock-out after failed attempts.
### Two-factor authentication
Password plus a code from a phone or a security key.
### Access rights (permissions)
Users get only the **privileges they need** (read, write, delete); students cannot change marks.
### Encryption
Data on disks and in transfer (HTTPS, VPN) cannot be read if intercepted.
### Firewalls and updates
Block unauthorised network access and close known security holes.
### Physical security and monitoring
Locked rooms, logging out, screen locks, **audit logs** of who accessed what.
:::

:::tip
Use the scenario: "The school should give teachers **write access** to marks and students **read-only access**, so students cannot change their grades."
:::

# Problems arising due to malware
@lo 12.1.3.2

:::compare
| Malware | Problem for individuals | Problem for organisations / society |
|---|---|---|
| Ransomware | Personal photos and documents encrypted | Hospitals and businesses stop working; ransom payments fund crime |
| Spyware / keyloggers | Stolen bank details, identity theft | Industrial espionage; leaked customer data → fines |
| Viruses / worms | Corrupted files, slow computer | Networks overloaded; cost of cleaning and downtime |
| Trojans / botnets | Computer controlled by criminals | Used in DDoS attacks against websites and e-government services |
| Adware / phishing apps | Pop-ups, fraud | Loss of customers' trust |
:::

# Privacy policies
@lo 12.1.3.3

:::definition Privacy policy
A public statement by a website, app or organisation explaining **what personal data it collects, why, how it is used, stored and protected, who it is shared with, and what rights the user has**.
:::

:::compare What a privacy policy should contain
| Section | Example |
|---|---|
| Data collected | Name, email, location, browsing history, cookies |
| Purpose | To provide the service, improve it, send newsletters, show adverts |
| Sharing | With payment providers, advertisers, government when required by law |
| Storage and security | Where data is stored, for how long, how it is protected (encryption) |
| User rights | See, correct, download or delete your data; withdraw consent; opt out of marketing |
| Cookies | Which cookies are used and how to refuse them |
| Contact | How to contact the data protection officer |
:::

:::warning
Many users accept privacy policies **without reading them** — they may agree to their data being sold or shared. Children's data needs special protection and parental consent.
:::
